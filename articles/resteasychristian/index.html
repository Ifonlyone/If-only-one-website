Rest Easy, Christian, There is no Apostasy

← All Articles
Bradford Smith August 2026  ·  If Only One

Doctrine matters. Our theology has legs.

I knew a woman once whose adult son made a profession of faith, got plugged into the local church, and began living an active life of faith. Her joy was palpable. Yet, at some point, he fell back into the drug addiction from which he’d been delivered. The woman was shattered. Because he was walking in willful sin and rejecting the fellowship he had in the church, she believed he must have lost his salvation. His damnation was practically certain—and her church’s interpretation of Hebrews 6:4–6 seemed to guarantee it.

It is a difficult text for sure, but I’ve come to loath such interpretations.

The Text

The author of Hebrews writes:

“For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm and holding him up to contempt.”

Hebrews 6:4–6 ESV

These verses have confounded Christians for centuries and many will trumpet, “Look at the plain reading of the text! It’s crystal clear.” The author of Hebrews establishes something as impossible regarding a particular group of people. This group of people possesses four characteristics:

  1. They’ve once been enlightened.
  2. They’ve tasted of the heavenly gift.
  3. They have shared in the Holy Spirit.
  4. They have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come.

This can only be believers, right? And so, these believers have done something: they’ve “fallen away”—that is, they’ve abandoned the faith they once held. As a result, what the author describes as “impossible” is their being restored to repentance.

This text, so the argument goes, clearly describes apostasy—the total abandonment of the faith by believers—and its strict judgment implies they have no hope of ever being restored. Therefore, the warning goes: Don’t fall away! Whatever you do! Others have done it, so take care not to follow them into apostasy.

My only response would be – excrement.

Principles First

Before we dig into this difficult text, let us establish a few essential hermeneutical principles:

There is no terror for the believer.

Any interpretation that instills dread or terror (as opposed to a healthy, reverent fear and awe of the Lord) is not from God. The author of Hebrews describes this plainly in chapter 12. Using a contrast, he describes the terror of Sinai—unbelievers confronted with the living God of the universe. Facing that reality, they begged not to hear another word: “Indeed, so terrifying was the sight that Moses said, ‘I tremble with fear’” (Hebrews 12:21). Conversely, he writes of believers, “But you have come to Mount Zion… to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem… to innumerable angels… to the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven… and to God… and to Jesus” (vv. 22–24). For the believer, there is no fear—only joy, peace, and rest.

If a teaching puts a burden upon the believer, it is not of God.

Jesus is in the business of removing burdens, not adding them. As He promises, “Come to me, all who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). In Christ, we have true rest. Therefore, we must summarily reject any teaching that imposes spiritual bondage.

There are no “tiers” or special classes of Christians.

Scripture leaves no room for spiritual categorization. In Christ, we are all “one body and one Spirit… one Lord, one faith, one baptism” (Ephesians 4:4–6).

Context rules.

We must always interpret the less clear, more difficult passages in light of the clearer, explicit teachings of Scripture.

Options for Interpretation

There are four distinct options for interpreting this passage:

  1. These are actual believers, and the text describes actual apostasy.
  2. These are actual believers, but the text describes something other than apostasy—such as a loss of heavenly reward rather than the loss of salvation itself.
  3. These are actual believers, but the text describes a hypothetical scenario. That is, the author does not expect these believers to actually fall away but issues this stern warning to prevent them from doing so. Think of it like this: after my son and I spend all day cleaning and organizing his room, I might say, “If we were to take a full can of trash and dump it back on the floor, it would be impossible to enjoy your room, and we’d have to start all that hard work over again.” I don’t actually expect him to do it, but the hypothetical warning reinforces the point.
  4. We’ll get to the fourth option shortly.

Context from Hebrews

Interestingly, we don’t have to venture outside the book of Hebrews to find strong contextual clues. Some of the first questions we should ask of any epistle are: Who is the audience? Who is this letter written about? The answers contained within Hebrews itself are both informative and decisive.

In chapter 2, verse 1, the author writes that “we” must pay attention. Clearly, whoever the audience is, the author includes himself in their group. In chapter 3, he addresses them as “holy brothers” and “brothers” (vv. 1, 12). These readers are Christians, and the author is a fellow believer.

We begin to see a sharp contrast in chapter 3 between “we” and “they.” The “they” are described as those who “were unable to enter [His rest] because of unbelief” (v. 19). The author consistently distinguishes between his audience and himself (believers) versus “they” (unbelievers). Chapter 4 continues this same contrast between “us” (v. 1) and “them” who were “not united by faith” (v. 2). Again: believers versus unbelievers.

He continues using these distinct pronouns into chapter 5, where we find even more contextual clues.

Chapter 5 closes with an admonition—almost a stern rebuke. The audience was not progressing in the faith as they should have been. They still needed spiritual milk instead of solid food and spiritual meat. They ought to have been teachers by this point, yet they lacked the maturity expected of them (Hebrews 5:12–14).

Chapter 6 then opens with a crucial “therefore.” Because of their salvation—and because they had not been growing as they ought—they must leave behind “the elementary doctrine of Christ” and “press on to maturity” (Hebrews 6:1). In other words: grow in your faith and in the knowledge of God. Verses 1 through 3 are once again framed with “we/us” language. For the author of Hebrews, this is a communal pursuit: “Let us,” he writes (v. 1). They are united as believers in this call, which brings us directly to our passage in question.

The subject shifts dramatically in verse 4. He transitions from “we/us” to “those”—specifically, those who possess the four characteristics mentioned earlier. It is “those” who are impossible to restore again to repentance. Not “you” or “we,” but “them.”

How do we know this shift is deliberate? Because he changes the subject right back in verse 9. After describing the imagery of judgment in verses 7 and 8, he reassures his readers: “Though we speak in this way, yet in your case, beloved, we feel sure of better things—things that belong to salvation” (v. 9). Verses 10 and 11 go on to provide unflinching assurance of the readers’ security in Christ.

“In other words: you mustn’t worry. Persist in the faith. Be diligent. Grow in grace—but above all, rest in Christ, our great Savior.”

Hebrews 10 and Willful Sin

Let’s address a similar—and clearer—text in Hebrews chapter 10:

“For if we go on sinning willfully after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a terrifying expectation of judgment.”

Hebrews 10:26–27a

Here we find a parallel sentiment. In chapter 6, there are those who “fall away.” In chapter 10, there are those who “go on sinning willfully.” Just as it is impossible to restore those in chapter 6 to repentance, here in chapter 10 “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins.” The reactionary reading concludes: We must not sin willfully! But I don’t know about you—if any willful sin disqualifies us from Christ’s sacrifice, we are all in serious trouble.

Let’s examine the surrounding context. What is this “willful sin,” anyway?

Thankfully, the author clarifies it for us. In verse 28, he references the Law of Moses and the severity of judgment for someone who rejected it. Then in verse 29, he writes:

“How much worse punishment, do you think, will be deserved by the one who has trampled underfoot the Son of God, and has profaned the blood of the covenant by which he was sanctified, and has outraged the Spirit of grace?”

Hebrews 10:29

What does it mean to profane the blood of the covenant and outrage the Spirit of grace?

Consider the consistent struggle of early Jewish believers: the temptation to return to legalistic law-keeping. The first major crisis in the early church (Acts 15) was confronting Judaizers—false teachers infiltrating the fellowship to push salvation by works. Paul wrote his epistle to the Galatians to address this exact threat. Here, the author of Hebrews writes to Jewish Christians whose primary temptation was to abandon grace and revert to Old Covenant ritualism.

That is how one tramples underfoot the Son of God. That is how one profanes the blood of the covenant and outrages the Spirit of grace: by abandoning the sufficiency of Christ and returning to law-keeping.

If that is the explicit warning in Hebrews 10—and it is—then that is the exact dynamic at play in Hebrews 6. They don’t simply stumble into sin; they walk away from the Gospel back to legalism. Why? Because that is what unconverted moralists do. Returning to works-righteousness is a constant temptation even for true believers, so how much more appealing is it to an unregenerate heart?

The Characteristics Revisited

Let us revisit the four characteristics. Surely these must describe Christians… right?

They have once been enlightened.

Surely someone who has been “enlightened” can only be a believer? A simple word study reveals a different conclusion. John writes: “There was the true Light [Jesus] which, coming into the world, enlightens every man” (John 1:9). No orthodox Christian claims Jesus saves every single person, yet John tells us Jesus enlightens every person. That is, Jesus reveals Himself to the world. Similarly, Paul writes that all people know God through creation and are therefore “without excuse” (Romans 1:20). In Hebrews, those who have been “enlightened” are those who have a cognitive knowledge of Jesus and the Gospel message. They have heard and understood it—but hearing and understanding say nothing about saving faith.

They have tasted of the heavenly gift.

The author writes in chapter 2 that Jesus “tasted death for everyone” (Hebrews 2:9). Yet Jesus is not still dead. He was in the grave for three days before His resurrection, but it was not a permanent condition. This echoes Jesus’ Parable of the Sower. The rocky soil and the weed-strangled soil yield a plant for a brief period. For a moment, they are indistinguishable from the good soil. It isn’t until trials come and the scorching sun wilts the plant that the shallow soil is exposed for what it is. Likewise, “those” people have only tasted of the heavenly gift and the things of God. They have not consumed them, nor have they been transformed by them.

They have shared in the Holy Spirit.

They have enjoyed a front-row seat to the work and presence of the Spirit, but they have not been regenerated, indwelt, or sealed by the Holy Spirit as all true believers are (Ephesians 1:13).

They have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come.

Again, they have tasted these things—the word of God and the supernatural power of God—only tasted. We must ask: if someone gave you a sample of the most delicious food on earth, and it was available to you in abundance, would you not feast on it?

This brings us firmly to Option 4: these are actually unbelievers, not believers. The author is describing unbelievers within the visible church who enjoy all the outward blessings of covenant community:

  • They hear the Gospel message.
  • They know who Jesus is.
  • They witness the work of the Spirit.
  • They participate in the spiritual environment of the church.

Yet they are not true believers. How do we know this? Because they fall away.

Scripture Provides Examples

Scripture provides ample evidence for this exact dynamic:

Demas: He traveled and labored alongside Paul on behalf of the Gospel (Colossians 4:14, Philemon 24). Yet Paul later writes that Demas, “in love with this present world, has deserted me and gone to Thessalonica” (2 Timothy 4:10). Demas fits all four characteristics, yet he walked away.

The Workers of Lawlessness: In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus describes a group who prophesy, cast out demons, and perform miracles in His name (Matthew 7:22). Notice He doesn’t say they merely claimed to do these things—they actually did them! That sounds remarkably like our four characteristics. Yet Jesus says to them, “I never knew you; depart from me” (v. 23). They were never true believers.

Judas Iscariot: Perhaps the most sobering example comes from the Twelve themselves. Jesus commissioned the Apostles and sent them out. Mark reports: “So they went out and proclaimed that people should repent” (Mark 6:12). They cast out demons and healed the sick (v. 13). This included Judas Iscariot. Judas preached, witnessed miracles, and likely performed signs. He was enlightened. He partook. He tasted… but he never knew Jesus. Was there ever an unbeliever with more of a front-row seat to the kingdom of God? Judas epitomizes Hebrews 6:4–6.

Revisiting the Principles

Recall the hermeneutical principles we established earlier. Let’s address them now in reverse order:

4. Context rules.

We can see clearly from the text—even within Hebrews alone—that the author is speaking of two distinct groups of people: believers and unbelievers. Both the immediate context and the broader biblical narrative drive us to Option 4. These individuals are not believers. The concept of a true believer “falling away” from ultimate salvation is foreign to Scripture. (Yes, I am speaking here of eternal security, historically known as the perseverance of the saints—that blessed “P” anchoring the Doctrines of Grace.) For robust biblical support, see John 6:37–39, John 10:28–29, Romans 8:38–39, Ephesians 1:13–14, Philippians 1:6, 1 Peter 1:4–5, and Hebrews 7:25.

3. There is no categorization of Christians.

There are no conservative or liberal Christians, no Black or white Christians, no Spirit-filled versus non-Spirit-filled Christians (by definition, every Christian is filled by the Holy Spirit), no favored Christians, and no special elite tier. All are equal in Christ—equally loved, equally blessed, and equally favored. Every seat at the Marriage Supper of the Lamb is a seat of honor. Every table is a head table. Every saint is highly favored. As such, no true Christian is in danger of ultimate apostasy. True believers will persevere to the end because that is what God enables His children to do. Just as an earthly father would not tear up the adoption papers of a child who stumbles, our Heavenly Father will never disown an adopted son or daughter of the King.

2. If a teaching puts a heavy spiritual burden on the believer, it is not of God.

I cannot imagine the crushing weight this false interpretation placed upon the mother whose son relapsed. If only he had enough faith… If only he did enough… If you just do enough, God will keep you. This is the Gospel flipped 180 degrees. The Gospel of Jesus Christ proclaims that He has accomplished everything required to transfer His people from death to life. He will never leave us nor forsake us. He will hold us fast. He will keep us—and even when we are faithless, He remains faithful. That is a message worth proclaiming!

1. There is no terror for the believer.

In our legalism and pietism, we default to fear. Yet there is profound rest in the absolute sovereignty of God over all things—especially over our salvation. I couldn’t ruin my salvation if I tried, and believe me, I’ve tried! How quickly we try to pull grace back into an “economy of merit”—the idea that if you just do these four things, or if you keep tasting, partaking, and participating, then you won’t fall away. But the ultimate destination of an economy of merit is hell, where punishment corresponds to the full measure of sin.

Here, the author describes a severe judgment for those who have been exposed to the things of God—the preaching of the Word, the visible work of the Spirit, the fellowship of the church—and have summarily rejected them. For them, humanly speaking, it is “impossible” to restore them again to repentance. But what does Scripture say? With God, all things are possible. It is not as if God looks at a hardened sinner and says, “Well, they’re just too far gone.” No! God will not lose a single one of His sheep.

Conclusion

Ultimately, when we view a passage like Hebrews 6:4–6 in its proper context, we find deep comfort rather than fear. No child of God has any reason to doubt their standing before the Father, regardless of the severity or length of their personal struggle. When rightly understood, this text reinforces that very assurance.

I would love to sit down with that grieving mother today and offer her these words of gospel encouragement—and I only wish her church had done the same.
